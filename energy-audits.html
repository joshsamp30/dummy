If your utility bill keeps creeping up, certain rooms are always too hot or too cold, or you've just bought a home and want a baseline, an energy audit is the smartest investment you can make. A real energy audit isn't a sales pitch with a checklist — it's a multi-hour, instrument-driven diagnostic that measures exactly how your home is performing. We use the same tools (and certifications) that the U.S. Department of Energy uses for its Home Energy Score program, including blower door testing, infrared thermal imaging, combustion analysis on every gas appliance and duct leakage testing under pressure.

Eric Kjelshus is a Building Performance Institute (BPI)-certified building analyst — one of a small number of contractors in the Kansas City metro qualified to perform a true comprehensive energy audit. Our typical residential audit takes 2–4 hours on-site, produces a written report ranking every problem we find by ROI, and qualifies your home for the federal 25C Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit of up to $1,200/year on insulation and air-sealing upgrades, plus utility rebates from Evergy and Spire.

What's Included in a Comprehensive Energy Audit

  1. BPI-certified safety testing on every gas-fired appliance — water heater, furnace, boiler, range — to verify no carbon-monoxide hazards exist and that combustion is complete and properly vented.
  2. Blower door test. A calibrated fan depressurises the home so we can measure exactly how leaky the building envelope is and locate the worst leaks.
  3. Infrared (thermal) imaging. While the blower door is running, we walk the home with a thermal camera and find missing insulation, hidden leaks, ductwork cavities and moisture problems invisible to the naked eye.
  4. Visual inspection of attic, basement, crawl space, mechanical room, ductwork, windows, doors, weatherstripping and insulation.
  5. Duct leakage test. Calibrated pressure testing of the duct system finds leaks that can waste up to 20% of every dollar you spend on heating and cooling.
  6. Combustion analysis on each gas appliance — measures CO output, oxygen levels and combustion efficiency.
  7. Energy modeling. We model your home in REM/Rate or Manual J to calculate expected savings from each recommended improvement.

What You Get

After the audit we deliver a written report with three tiers of recommendations: low-cost/no-cost (caulk, weatherstrip, programmable thermostat), moderate-cost high-ROI (attic insulation, duct sealing, basement-band insulation), and major upgrades (HVAC replacement, geothermal, window replacement). Each recommendation includes the estimated cost, expected annual savings and the available federal/state/utility incentives. We don't pressure you to pick us for the work — but we are happy to quote any of it.

The 25C Insulation Tax Credit

The Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (Section 25C of the IRS code) lets you take a federal tax credit equal to 30% of the cost of qualifying insulation, air-sealing and energy-efficient HVAC upgrades, up to $1,200/year for insulation and air sealing — every year — through 2032. Heat pumps, biomass stoves and certain other equipment have their own separate $2,000 credit. We help you identify what qualifies and document it for your tax filing.

Utility Rebates Through Evergy & Spire

As an Evergy Authorized Contractor, we offer customers exclusive access to Evergy rebates: $550–$800 on qualifying central air conditioning, up to $200 on mini-splits, and significant savings on ground-source heat pumps. Spire (natural gas) offers complementary rebates on high-efficiency furnaces and water heaters. We handle the rebate paperwork for you.

How to Prepare for an Energy Audit

  • Clear access to attics, basements, crawl spaces, mechanical rooms and HVAC equipment.
  • Pull your utility bills from the last 12 months — gas and electric.
  • Make a list of any drafts, cold spots, condensation problems or comfort complaints.
  • Note any recent renovations, additions or HVAC changes.

Common Issues We Find

  • Disconnected duct boots dumping conditioned air into attics and crawl spaces
  • Missing or compressed attic insulation in the corners and along the eaves
  • Open chases and plumbing penetrations that allow air to flow freely between floors
  • Recessed cans without insulation contact ratings (IC) leaking air into the attic
  • Combustion appliances backdrafting into the home — a CO hazard
  • Ducts in unconditioned spaces with no insulation
  • Bath and dryer exhaust fans terminating in attics rather than outside
  • Massive window air leakage from failed weatherstripping
